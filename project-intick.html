Asset managers trading block-size listed derivatives get worse outcomes in as much as 70% of cases, because off-screen liquidity is opaque and the market can move against them mid-search. Intick built a marketplace where buy-side firms match directly with counterparties or send RFQs to liquidity providers they couldn't otherwise reach, all aggregated into a single lit order book so traders work within the tick, not against it. There was no existing pattern for most of this: showing live liquidity across counterparties, making residual amounts and match status legible at a glance, or designing for users with decades of muscle memory from legacy terminals. Anything that broke convention had to earn its place.

Strategy & concepting

This started as strategy, not screens. Alongside the founder and CPO, I helped shape the platform's core idea: what the ladder could look like, how offers would move between parties, what would make this different from the terminals traders had used for decades. The strategy evolved with us as we went. Early sessions were rough paper sketches: price tickers, a basic buy/sell ladder, first attempts at showing liquidity.

Early iterations

I joined alongside an agency dev team already deep into market and exchange integrations. With the founder and tech lead, we formed a quad, iterating on key ideas and screens while I built a design system in parallel. The goal was a demo credible enough for partner and investor buy-in: bootstrapped account creation first, then the first real concepts, parent cards on the left, order book scrolling horizontally, tested in light and dark mode.

Testing: who and how

Standard usability heuristics weren't enough, we needed people who understood block trading to tell us when something broke their mental model versus when it was just unfamiliar. We ran structured sessions with named buy-side traders, portfolio managers, sales traders, and execution brokers, using pre-built dev accounts for different states. Every session was recorded, clipped, timestamped, and tagged into a friction-point-to-solution log.

Recurring issues: order book orientation broke convention ("I'd typically see all the bids on one side and the ask on the other"), the cold-start state read as broken rather than empty, and adding an order felt unfamiliar. Each finding fed back into design: the parent/child card model, retested, landed far better ("makes a lot more sense, I can really see what's going on here"); the order book was restructured to match convention; the empty state got clear messaging; a lighter tweaks pass stripped visual noise and repetition.

Refining the trading language

As the product matured, we moved from agency-supported to a dedicated in-house team, with me on the front end and running proper sprints for the first time. We tested five trading scenarios end-to-end to unify the summary box, one of the hardest interaction problems on the platform, since a single trade could be aggressive, passive, combined, or residual, and all of it had to be legible at a glance. Traders wanted trades combined rather than suggested separately, valued repeated info as a safety check, and defaulted to aggressive first. This meant refining the visual language: Summary Box 2.0, a traffic-light system for order states, a buy/sell colour convention, full dark/light mode, a visual language that hadn't existed before.

Building out the design system

Here the work became a proper system: tokens, components, and structural concepts compared side by side before committing to one that scaled across every tested scenario. We pressure-tested it live with the Intick committee, a group of ex-bankers, through a full sales demo covering the entire loop: checking markets, sending an RFQ, taking and rejecting responses, adjusting to a market move, cancelling and re-sending. It held up.

Outcome

The result gives traders what they said they needed most: real-time clarity on every order, in a language built for how they actually work.
